# Price every decision, cap every day

Blast Agents should sell **decisions, not days**. The user picks a model tier and a daily decision budget. The vault pays that budget once a day in SUI, and the on-chain `daily_fee_cap` becomes the user's own signed spending limit rather than a platform price. Offer seven curated models in three price tiers: **Frontier at $0.04 per decision** (Claude Sonnet 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol, Grok 4.x, Gemini 3.5 Flash), **Standard at $0.015** (DeepSeek V4 Pro) and **Budget at $0.004** (Claude Haiku 5.5 or GPT-6 Luna, plus DeepSeek V4.1 Flash). These prices sit 2.2–7× above even the uncached cost of each model, so a referred decision still keeps **25–56% of revenue after the referrer's fixed 30%**. That pricing rule is what makes the owner's 30% referral share safe. Under it, self-referral is only a 30% discount and can never be net-negative, so referral anti-abuse can be light. No minimum deposit works because a $20 vault can buy five Budget decisions a day for 2 cents. Small vaults cost almost nothing to serve and pay in proportion. Frontier users carry the $4,000–4,500 of monthly fixed costs. The evidence for choosing models is thin. Alpha Arena's two public seasons crowned different winners (Qwen3 Max, then Grok 4.20), and academic benchmarks find that the agent design matters more than the model. So the list should be curated for brand recognition, strict-JSON reliability, privacy routing and cost, not for "trading skill". Three things stay uncertain. Every price outside Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol is unverified, because openrouter.ai was unreachable during this research. Real cache hit rates are unmeasured. Nobody has tested whether users accept per-decision pricing. The design therefore includes a rule that re-prices automatically from measured cost.

## Alpha Arena's winners flipped each season, so curate for reliability, not "skill"

The only widely covered live LLM trading contest is Nof1's Alpha Arena. It does not support claiming that any model is a good trader. In Season 1 (18 Oct – 3 Nov 2025, crypto perps on Hyperliquid, $10k of real money per model), **Qwen3 Max won with +22.3% from only 43 trades**, and DeepSeek V3.1 came second at about +4.9% ([RootData](https://www.rootdata.com/news/412456); [iWeaver](https://www.iweaver.ai/blog/alpha-arena-ai-trading-season-1-results/)). The four US models each lost more than 30%, with GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro worst ([ForkLog](https://forklog.com/en/four-out-of-six-ai-models-suffer-losses-in-trading-tournament/)). Season 1.5 moved to US equities, and a "Mystery Model", later revealed as **Grok 4.20, won with about +12%** ([GNcrypto](https://www.gncrypto.news/news/mystery-model-alpha-arena-season-1-5-winner/); [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/elon-musks-grok-4-20-123855766.html)). Qwen collapsed on equities, and sources disagree on who came second ([AIbase](https://news.aibase.com/news/23442)). No 2026 season has public results, and the archived leaderboard ends on 12 December 2025 ([TradeRank](https://www.traderank.ai/blog/alpha-arena-alternatives-2026)). With one two-week run per season and dozens of trades per model, the lead flipping from Qwen to Grok looks like variance and regime fit, not stable skill. The academic work agrees. Agent Market Arena found that **"model backbones contributed less to outcome variation"** than agent frameworks did ([arXiv 2510.11695](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11695)). A 2026 review calls StockBench results "model-, window- and cost-specific" ([arXiv 2609.04917](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.04917v1)). No benchmark covers memecoin launches on a bonding curve, which is Blast's actual task.

What products actually ship is more useful than who won the contests. Senpi, a Hyperliquid agent priced from $50/month, lets users bring **ChatGPT, Claude or Grok** ([Senpi](https://senpi.ai/)). ElizaOS lists Anthropic and OpenRouter first among its LLM plugins, and its OpenRouter plugin defaults to Gemini Flash ([ElizaOS docs](https://docs.elizaos.ai/plugins/llm); [ElizaOS OpenRouter plugin](https://docs.elizaos.ai/plugin-registry/llm/openrouter)). An open Eliza issue asks for first-class DeepSeek, xAI, Kimi and z.ai providers ([GitHub #24097](https://github.com/elizaOS/eliza/issues/24097)). On OpenRouter itself, **DeepSeek V4.1 Flash leads all models by tokens**, and seven of the top ten are open-weight ([tokenmaxxing](https://tokenmaxxing.com/openrouter-rankings)). Giza, Almanak, Wayfinder and HeyAnon do not disclose their models ([Crypto Daily](https://cryptodaily.co.uk/2026/05/top-4-ai-trading-solutions-redefining-on-chain-execution-in-2026)). Two groups of models cover the market. Claude, GPT and Grok are the brands users know. DeepSeek and Gemini Flash are the cost-driven builder defaults.

Every model must pass four gates before it appears in the UI. First, an OpenRouter endpoint that advertises structured outputs, called with `require_parameters: true` so that OpenRouter never falls back to a provider that only does plain JSON or text ([OpenRouter provider selection](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/routing/provider-selection)). Second, a zero-data-retention route, using `zdr: true` and `data_collection: "deny"` ([OpenRouter ZDR](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/privacy/zdr)). OpenRouter counts **26 providers that retain prompts**, so this setting matters ([OpenRouter providers](https://openrouter.ai/providers)). Third, a non-China host for DeepSeek and Qwen. Fourth, an arena run that measures JSON validity, latency and real cost per decision. Use `eu.openrouter.ai` to keep traffic in Europe ([OpenRouter Sovereign AI](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/features/sovereign-ai)). One routing detail carries over from the earlier unit-economics work. Setting `provider.order` disables the sticky routing that produces cache hits ([OpenRouter prompt caching](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/best-practices/prompt-caching.md)). Restrict hosts by excluding providers rather than ordering them, and test that the cache hit rate survives the restriction. Reasoning-by-default models (Grok, GPT "Sol"-class, DeepSeek reasoning variants) can multiply output billing. Set reasoning to low or off, and meter the real `completion_tokens`. Marketing may honestly say a model "competed in Alpha Arena". It may never say a model "beats the market".

## Price each decision at 2.2–7× uncached cost so a 30% referral never loses money

The pricing rule follows from the owner's fixed 30% referral share. If a decision sells for P and costs C, a referred decision keeps 0.7P − C. Requiring a contribution margin m on referred revenue gives **P ≥ C / (0.7 − m)**. At m = 30% that means pricing at 2.5× cost. At m = 40% it means 3.3×. Set each tier so that the _uncached_ cost (the launch-day worst case, when few vaults share a cache) still clears about 25%, and the expected cached cost clears 40% or more. Cost per decision uses the repo's standard shape (5,000 shared prefix tokens, 1,000 vault tokens, 500 output tokens) plus OpenRouter's credit fee. The fee is **5.5% on the standard plan** ([Layer3Labs](https://www.layer3labs.io/guides/openrouter-pricing)) and 5% for crypto top-ups ([OpenRouter blog](https://openrouter.ai/blog/simplifying-our-platform-fee)). The tables below use 5.5% to stay conservative. Sonnet 5.5's cache read is $0.10/M after the October cut, and its 5-minute cache write is $2.50/M ([SmartScope](https://smartscope.blog/en/blog/claude-sonnet-5-5-cache-read-price-cut-2026/)).

| Sonnet 5.5 / GPT-6.1 Sol | $0.0179 ($0.0104 at 80% cache hits) | $0.040     | 2.2× (3.8×)  | 55% (74%)            | 25% (44%)                    |

These margins sit in the range AI businesses report. ICONIQ's survey puts the **average AI product gross margin at about 45% in 2025 and 52–53% in 2026** ([SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/have-ai-gross-margins-really-turned-the-corner/)). Bessemer's "Shooting Stars" run near 60% ([CloudZero](https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/ai-gross-margin)). Frontier at $0.04 is close to the old flat plan: 25 Frontier decisions cost $1.00 a day, against the $0.99 per 30 decisions in SPEC 1b. Pricing should launch high and fall as measured caching improves, because price cuts please users and increases anger them. The table also shows which costs the margins are exposed to. A Frontier model that silently bills 2,000 reasoning tokens, or a DeepSeek host that charges twice DeepSeek's own endpoint, wipes out the referred margin. So the rule has to run continuously, not only at launch. The worker should compute each model's trailing measured cost per decision from OpenRouter's `usage` fields and raise an alarm when the referred margin, 0.7 − C/P, falls below 20%. The response is to re-price that tier or pull the model. Pricing per tier rather than per model keeps the UI simple. A model whose measured cost drifts out of its tier's band moves to a different tier.

Gas is the one cost that should not carry a margin. Blast's own mainnet transactions show swap-like PTBs costing anywhere from a rebate to **about 8M MIST**, roughly $0.007 at SUI ≈ $0.87 ([Blast mainnet e2e log](https://github.com/interest-protocol/blast-v2-contracts/blob/main/deployments/sui/mainnet/2026-10-20-blast-presale-versioned-e2e.json)). Pass the measured gas of each executed trade through at cost, as its own line, and leave it out of the referral base. Per-trade fees reward the operator when the agent trades more, and here the operator also runs the agent. Trojan and Maestro charge **about 1% per trade** ([DEXTools](https://www.dextools.io/tutorials/maestro-vs-trojan-vs-banana-gun)), which at one turnover a day costs a vault roughly 700% a year. Charging gas at cost removes the operator's reason to make the agent trade more. The platform then earns from the activity level the user chose, not from trades the agent decides to make.

## Users buy a daily decision budget, and tiny vaults buy tiny budgets

The market uses five fee models, and each one fails a no-minimum, user-chosen-activity product in a predictable way. **Flat subscriptions** turn into huge percentage drags on small balances. Acorns' $3/month is 36% a year on $100, and reviewers name that drag as the main complaint ([NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/investing/reviews/acorns)). Cryptohopper ($29–129/month) and 3Commas ($15–160/month) have the same shape ([PickMyTrade](https://blog.pickmytrade.io/cryptohopper-vs-3commas-vs-diy-bots-honest-2026-guide/); [GetApp](https://www.getapp.com/all-software/a/3commas)). **AUM fees** scale with the balance but ignore activity. Wealthfront charges 0.25% a year and requires $500 to open ([NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/investing/learn/betterment-vs-wealthfront)). **Performance fees** align best with users but earn nothing in drawdowns. Hyperliquid fixes vault leaders at **10% of profits above a high-water mark** ([Hyperliquid docs](https://hyperliquid.gitbook.io/hyperliquid-docs/hypercore/vaults/for-vault-leaders)). **Per-trade fees** reward overtrading. **Usage-based hybrids** are now the norm: Orb finds that **95% of AI-agent companies use hybrid pricing and 91% have a usage component** ([Orb](https://www.withorb.com/blog/2026-state-of-ai-agent-pricing-models-trends-and-whats-working)). The owner's decisions point at pure usage pricing. Users choose an activity level, pay for exactly that, and face no floor. The unit is the LLM decision, the thing that actually drives cost.

Concretely, the user sets three things at Start: a model (which sets the tier price), an activity level in decisions per day (presets such as Watch 5, Steady 20 and Active 60, plus any custom number with no platform maximum), and the matching on-chain fee cap, which they sign. The daily budget is decisions × tier price, plus an estimate of trade gas. The current contract charges once per UTC day, so v1 should **prepay that budget at the start of each day** through `charge_fee`. The operator then runs at most that many decisions. If triggers fire less often than the budget allows, the unused decisions become an off-chain credit that reduces the next day's charge, and the previous day's measured gas is settled the same way. Charging less needs no on-chain change, and no funds move back from the treasury. Only the final day's unused budget is forfeited when a user leaves, and the UI must say so. Prepaying means the platform never carries credit exposure. A user who empties the vault mid-day has already paid for that day. A raise in activity takes effect the next day, unless the v2 contract below allows charges within the day. When the budget runs out, the agent stops calling the LLM, but deterministic exits (stop-loss and take-profit rules that need no model call) keep running. A user who chose five decisions is then never trapped in a falling position. The user's own decision budget is the cap. Two platform guards stay, and neither limits users. The existing `PlatformSpendGuard` kill switch, which stops on OpenRouter 402 errors, serves as an anomaly brake for runaway loops. The gas and fixed costs of an idle vault must stay near zero, which batched reads already provide.

The table shows both the trade-off and how to protect small users without capping them. Under the old flat $0.99/day, a $250 vault paid **144.5% a year**, and smaller vaults were not allowed at all. Under per-decision pricing the same vault pays 2.9% on Budget-5 or 117% on Frontier-20, depending on what its owner picked. The UI should open on the highest preset whose projected fee stays at or below about 10% a year of the current balance. Above 25% a year it should show an amber warning. Above 100% it should show a red warning with "the agent must make X% a month before you break even". All three are defaults and disclosures. None of them blocks the user. This follows how regulators frame costs. MiFID II requires an illustration of the cumulative effect of costs on return ([ESMA Q&A](https://www.esma.europa.eu/publications-data/questions-answers/2136)), and MiCA Article 81 requires portfolio managers to assess suitability and send periodic statements ([ESMA MiCA Art. 81](https://www.esma.europa.eu/publications-and-data/interactive-single-rulebook/mica/article-81-providing-advice-crypto-assets)). Whether a user-configured agent counts as portfolio management is still a question for counsel. On dust-sized trades, gas and Blast's 150 bps fee matter more than the decision price. The risk check should refuse buys where gas would exceed about 1% of the trade. That protects users, and it does not cap activity.

With no minimum deposit, the trial has to change. Today `create(…, free_days)` gives three free days on-chain. With user-chosen activity, a free day is an uncapped cost: one free day at 1,000 Frontier decisions costs about $18 in model fees. zkLogin wallets cost nothing to create, so a sybil farmer can repeat that indefinitely. Pass `free_days = 0` always. Replace the trial with a small off-chain credit, for example $0.25 of decisions per wallet whose vault holds funds. That is about 60 Budget decisions or six Frontier ones, at a worst-case cost of a few cents. Free credit never earns referral payouts. Fixed costs set a separate constraint from the per-decision margin. At $4,400 a month, the platform needs about **150,000 non-referred Frontier decisions a month** (roughly 200 vaults at 25 a day, at $0.0296 contribution each), or about 1.4 million Budget decisions. Small users are therefore growth and referral fuel, and they are not a loss, because every decision they buy has a positive margin. Frontier users pay for the company.

## The fee cap is a user-signed spending limit, enforced per day in native SUI

An on-chain fee cap is the most the operator's key can take from a vault in a given period. The vault contract enforces it, independently of whether the operator behaves honestly. It is the failure bound. If the KMS key is compromised or the billing code has a bug, the loss is at most the cap per period, until the owner revokes. The industry learned this the expensive way with unlimited ERC-20 approvals. Dedaub estimated **more than $6.5B exposed through unlimited approvals** on the top-20 Ethereum tokens in 2024 ([Matcha](https://blog.matcha.xyz/article/permit2)). Current practice has converged on bounded, periodic, revocable permissions. Coinbase/Base Spend Permissions specify an `allowance` per `period` with start and end times, and report the remaining allowance per period ([CDP reference](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/coinbase-wallet/reference/onchain-contracts/spend-permissions.md)). MetaMask's ERC-7715 `native-token-periodic` permission resets a `periodAmount` every `periodDuration`, such as 86,400 seconds ([MetaMask docs](https://docs.metamask.io/smart-accounts-kit/development/guides/advanced-permissions/use-permissions/native-token/)). Safe's Allowance Module grants an amount per reset period ([Candide](https://docs.candide.dev/wallet/plugins/allowance/)). Squads spending limits do not let unused amounts carry over ([Squads API](https://developers.squads.so/squads-api/api-reference/v1/endpoint/spending-limits/post.md)). Taken together, best practice has eight parts: a bounded amount, a period that resets with no carry-over, a single fixed payee, changes only by the owner, instant revocation, an optional expiry, an event for every charge, and a denomination in the native asset, so that no oracle can move the limit.

Blast's vault already meets most of these. `charge_fee` pays at most `daily_fee_cap` MIST, once per UTC day, only to the owner-approved `vault.treasury`. It never back-charges skipped days, never sells tokens to pay, and stops working on pause or revoke. Only `set_fee_terms(&OwnerCap)` can change the terms, and `scripts/check_trade_cap_surface.py` checks in CI that this stays the only outflow. Keep the cap in MIST. A USD cap would need Pyth on the one outflow path, with staleness and confidence handling, and a manipulated price would let the operator pull more SUI ([Sui oracle safety](https://docs.sui.io/onchain-finance/oracles/oracle-safety)). Convert USD to SUI off-chain at billing time, and set the cap at **about 3× the chosen daily budget at the approval price**. The earlier unit-economics model estimated that a 2× cap binds for about 20% of vaults within 180 days after a 50% fall in SUI, and a 3× cap for about 5%. The existing `feeCapHeadroom` re-approval prompt at 80% still applies. Under the user-chosen model the cap does a second job: it is the activity level the user signed for. Moving to a higher activity level that exceeds the cap needs a new `set_fee_terms` signature, the same consent pattern Hyperliquid uses for capped, revocable builder-fee approvals ([Hyperliquid builder codes](https://hyperliquid.gitbook.io/hyperliquid-docs/trading/builder-codes)). Moving to a lower level needs no signature.

Three contract refinements would bring the vault fully in line with best practice. None of them loosens the bound, but each one touches the `charge_fee` path. Each therefore needs the owner's sign-off, an update to the CI guard, tests, and the audit re-review the earlier work budgeted for. None of them should be published without explicit approval. The first and most useful is a **cumulative allowance per period**: replace "one charge per day" with `charged_today + amount ≤ daily_fee_cap`, resetting each UTC day. That is the Spend Permissions model. It lets the operator charge in smaller prepaid blocks during the day, so mid-day activity increases take effect at once and nobody forfeits a full day's prepayment. The daily maximum is unchanged, and simple shared-object calls cost about $0.0001 in gas. The second is an optional **balance-relative guard set by the owner**: `amount ≤ min(daily_fee_cap, sui_balance × max_fee_bps / 10_000)`, where 0 means off. Because the owner sets it, it is a protection users can choose for themselves, not a platform cap. It needs no oracle, because the vault knows its own SUI balance. It only ever lowers the bound. The third is an optional **terms expiry day**, after which `charge_fee` aborts until the owner re-approves. This matches the "end" field in Permit2 and Spend Permissions ([Eco on Permit2](https://eco.com/support/en/articles/12005545-what-is-permit2-a-2026-guide-to-token-approvals)). The UI should always show the cap three ways: in SUI, in USD, and as "about X% a year of your current balance".

## Pay 30% of collected service fees, not volume, and self-referral stops mattering

Every comparable programme pays a share of **fees actually collected**, not of volume. Axiom pays "30% of net fee" on direct referrals ([uwuu.ai](https://uwuu.ai/blog/axiom-trade-review)). BONKbot pays 30% in month one, 20% in month two and 10% for life ([Lyra Terminal](https://lyra-terminal.com/trading-bots/bonkbot)). DipCoin on Sui pays 25% of fees paid ([CommunityOne](https://communityone.io/pt/servers/1352162121760112660/dipcoin-official/news/dipcoin-25-percent-referral-program-2026-04-16/)). dYdX starts affiliates at **30% of taker fees, rising to 50%** with referred volume ([dYdX](https://www.dydx.xyz/blog/affiliate-tiers)). Hyperliquid pays 10% net of the referee discount ([Hyperliquid referrals](https://hyperliquid.gitbook.io/hyperliquid-docs/referrals)). OKX pays up to 50% for life, settled hourly ([OKX rules](https://okx-api.aperture.finance/help/okx-affiliate-program-rules)). A flat 30% therefore sits at the top of what bots pay and level with dYdX's base rate. The owner's phrase, "30% of revenue on the volume their referrals bring", should be implemented as **30% of the net service revenue that referred users actually pay**. Net service revenue means decision fees collected through `charge_fee` (the `FeeCharged` events), minus the gas pass-through line, free credits and any referee discount. A share of trading volume would pay referrers whenever the agent churns. No programme pays that way, and it would bring back the overtrading incentive. If Blast's own protocol trade fee is later counted as "revenue on volume", count it only after the MiCA opinion, and only as 30% of Blast's _net_ share. At the documented defaults that is 22.5 bps of volume (30% of Blast's half of 150 bps) ([Blast launchpad docs](https://github.com/interest-protocol/blast-v2-contracts/blob/main/docs/llms/launchpad.md)). The live values read as zero at deployment, so read them on-chain first. MiCA Article 81 bars crypto portfolio managers from accepting third-party benefits, so the agent's decision logic must never see referral or fee economics ([Springlex](https://www.springlex.eu/en/packages/mica/mica-regulation/article-81/)).

The pricing rule makes the economics safe by construction. Every tier keeps at least 25% of a referred decision's price after the payout, so the platform never pays out more than a decision earns. That changes the main abuse threat. A referrer who refers their own second wallet collects 30% of their own fees, which amounts to a 30% discount. They still pay 0.7P, which is above cost, so self-referral cannot be net-negative. Wash volume is not possible either, because referees do not trade by hand. The operator's agent trades within on-chain limits, and the referral base excludes trading volume. The remaining exploit is farming free credit, which is closed by paying nothing on free credit and offering no sign-up bonuses. pump.fun shows the risk of bonuses: it pays its up-to-$50 referral rewards in instalments after review, from a capped budget ([Airdrop Alert](https://airdropalert.com/airdrops/pump-fun-referral-rewards/)). Anti-abuse can therefore stay proportionate. Bind one referrer per owner wallet, permanently. Block self-referral by address and by funding source (the referee's first funding must not come from the referrer within two hops). Use device and IP signals off-chain. Require the referrer to own a vault with at least one paid day, a cheap gate in the spirit of Hyperliquid's $10k-volume requirement. Hold the first payout for 30 days. Claw back unpaid accruals on proven fraud. Above a monthly payout threshold (for example $1,000, an unsourced placeholder), collect KYC and tax details. Binance and OKX both reserve the right to amend or withhold rewards for abuse, and the terms should say the same ([Binance](https://www.binance.com/en/support/announcement/detail/cd17b963330c47e189e374cf8d845102)). Professional fraud detection fuses device, funding-source and on-chain cluster signals ([Track360](https://www.track360.io/blog/crypto-affiliate-fraud-detection-operator-playbook-2026)). That is worth adding once payouts become large.

The mechanics should leave the vault package untouched. **Attribution:** store a `?ref=` code in the browser for about 30 days. At Start, the owner signs a Sui personal message binding their address to the referrer, which keeps the owner-consent pattern without a Move change. The first binding wins and cannot be changed, and it is keyed by owner wallet, so opening a second vault cannot switch referrers. **Accrual:** for each `FeeCharged` event, record `(referrer, vault, day, service_mist, payout_mist = 30%)`, idempotent per vault-day and verifiable against the chain. **Payout:** pay weekly in SUI from the treasury, in batched PTBs (the existing 250-per-PTB batching), with a minimum of about 1 SUI that otherwise rolls over. Every batch is dry-run first and signed only with human approval, as CLAUDE.md requires for any movement of funds. A later option is a separate `referral_rewards` package in which the treasury deposits weekly balances that referrers claim themselves, the pattern Cetus uses for partner balances ([Cetus partner swap](https://cetus-1.gitbook.io/cetus-developer-docs/developer/via-sdk-v2/sdk-modules/cetusprotocol-sui-clmm-sdk/partner-swap)). It must stay a separate package from the vault. **Structure:** launch with a single level only. BehindMLM flags Trojan's five-level scheme as an MLM and securities red flag ([BehindMLM](https://behindmlm.com/mlm-reviews/trojan-review-telegram-bot-crypto-securities-commodities-fraud)). Referrers may give part of their 30% back to referees, as OKX lets affiliates do, but never on top of it. **Duration:** 12 months per referee from their first paid day is the bounded default, in line with bots that decay their share. The pricing rule makes a lifetime 30% affordable per decision too, and lifetime is the CEX norm, so the choice is a marketing call rather than a margin one. **Disclosure:** show referees "your referrer earns 30% of your service fee". Outgoing payments to third parties fall under MiFID II Art. 24(9)-style disclosure duties rather than the Art. 24(8) ban ([ESMA Q&A 1861](https://www.esma.europa.eu/publications-data/questions-answers/1861)), and disclosure costs nothing.

## Conclusion

The owner's constraints looked like they conflicted: 30% to referrers, no minimum deposit, no platform cap, and still a healthy margin. They fit together once the unit of sale moves from the day to the decision. A per-decision price set at 2.2× or more of each model's uncached cost turns the 30% referral share into a predictable discount. It turns self-referral into a harmless edge case, and it lets a $10 vault and a $10,000 vault use the same product honestly at different intensities. The on-chain cap changes meaning in the same move. It stops encoding the platform's price and becomes the user's signed spending limit, which is the role Spend Permissions and ERC-7715 already give caps elsewhere.

Two things will decide whether this works, and neither is settled by the research. The first is measured cost. Every non-Anthropic and non-OpenAI price here is unverified, and the margins in the referred column are what an unbilled reasoning token or an expensive host eats first. The re-pricing alarm therefore matters more than the launch prices. The second is whether users actually choose sensible activity levels. Pump.fun's record of about half of wallets losing money in a typical month ([crypto.news](https://crypto.news/pump-fun-data-shows-49-of-march-traders-in-the-red-as-platform-locks-fees/)) suggests that many will not, so the defaults and the %-per-year warnings matter more for retention than any discount. Before user deposits open, the highest-value next steps are a single pull of OpenRouter's `/api/v1/models` endpoint (blocked during this research) to verify prices and JSON support, and the MiCA opinion on whether a user-configured agent is portfolio management.
